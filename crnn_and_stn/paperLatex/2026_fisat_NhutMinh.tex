\begin{abstract}
Low-Resolution License Plate Recognition (LRLPR) remains a critical challenge in surveillance due to severe motion blur, long capture distances, and lossy video compression. While traditional pipelines treat image restoration and character recognition as decoupled stages, this work investigates the synergy of end-to-end joint optimization. We introduce MF-SR-OCR, a unified Multi-Frame Super-Resolution and Recognition framework tailored for low-resolution video tracks. The proposed architecture integrates per-frame Spatial Transformer Network (STN) alignment, deformable (DCNv2) cross-frame feature alignment, pixel-supervised super-resolution, attention-guided multi-frame fusion, and layout-constrained Connectionist Temporal Classification (CTC) decoding, all trained under a single objective and stabilized by Exponential Moving Average (EMA) weight smoothing. Systematic ablation isolates the contribution of the restoration path itself, and of its upscaling factor, against a multi-frame CRNN+STN baseline. Evaluated across multiple seeds on the authentic, non-synthetic tracks of a recent LRLPR benchmark, MF-SR-OCR improves on that baseline and is the most stable configuration we trained. The learned super-resolution branch does maintain a reconstruction loss below bilinear interpolation throughout training; a controlled ablation that removes the restoration path entirely, however, matches this accuracy at a fraction of the computational cost, so the branch yields no measurable recognition gain on this benchmark. Our findings locate the measurable improvement in geometric rectification, domain-matched degradation, constrained decoding and weight averaging rather than in super-resolution, and highlight the critical necessity of multi-seed evaluation when benchmarking subtle architectural iterations.
\keywords{license plate recognition \and low-resolution image \and super resolution \and spatial transformer network \and multi-frame fusion.}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Automatic License Plate Recognition (ALPR) is close to solved on sharp images: convolutional-recurrent recognizers transcribe a cropped plate end to end without character segmentation \cite{shi2017crnn}, provided the test images resemble the training ones \cite{laroca2022cross}. Surveillance footage breaks that condition. Long capture distances, low sensor resolution, motion blur, and video compression shrink character strokes to a few pixels, and accuracy on genuinely low-resolution plates falls by more than an order of magnitude \cite{nascimento2025toward}. A plate also counts as correct only when all seven characters are right, so one confused glyph invalidates the whole track. Low-Resolution License Plate Recognition (LRLPR) is therefore treated as a separate problem, formalized by a recent benchmark of $20{,}000$ tracks that pair five low-resolution with five high-resolution captures of the same plate under real, not synthetic, degradation; its winning entry reaches only $82.13\%$ \cite{laroca2026icpr}.

The usual remedy is a super-resolution (SR) stage placed before the recognizer. Plate-specific models improve on generic restorers using attention with sub-pixel upsampling \cite{nascimento2023attention}, layout and character priors \cite{nascimento2024lcdnet}, or embedding-level consistency between high-resolution and super-resolved plates \cite{sendjasni2025embedding}. A second line exploits temporal redundancy, since noise and sub-pixel offsets differ across the frames of a track. Deformable alignment established this idea for video restoration \cite{wang2019edvr}, and multi-frame methods report the largest gains for plates: from $31.1\%$ to $44.7\%$ by voting over five super-resolved frames \cite{nascimento2025toward}, and from $14.04\%$ to $86.44\%$ with optical-flow-based restoration \cite{na2025mflpr2}.

Three gaps remain. First, restoration and recognition are trained separately: the SR module minimizes a pixel loss and passes its output to a frozen recognizer, so it never sees the recognition error. Second, the criteria used to supervise it track recognition accuracy only loosely---a mismatch reported in the plate super-resolution literature \cite{nascimento2025toward} and one that motivates layout- and character-aware objectives \cite{nascimento2024lcdnet,sendjasni2025embedding}. Restorers built on pretrained generative priors carry a further risk, having been reported to introduce artifacts and distortions on severely degraded plates \cite{na2025mflpr2}. Third, LRLPR variants are usually compared with single runs; in our own experiments the gaps between competing variants turn out to be no larger than the variation between random seeds, which makes such comparisons unreadable.

To close these three gaps at once, this paper presents MF-SR-OCR, a unified multi-frame framework in which restoration and recognition are trained under a single objective and evaluated under a protocol that makes the noise floor explicit. It is built on the benchmark's multi-frame CRNN+STN baseline ($77.00\%$). Five low-resolution frames are rectified per frame by a Spatial Transformer Network \cite{jaderberg2015stn}, aligned across frames by identity-initialized DCNv2 kernels \cite{zhu2019dcnv2}, super-resolved $\times 2$, fused by attention over a GroupNorm residual backbone \cite{wu2018groupnorm}, and decoded by a BiLSTM head under CTC \cite{graves2006ctc} constrained to the two plate layouts in the corpus. Training uses one objective, $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CTC}} + \lambda_{\mathrm{SR}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SR}}$ with $\lambda_{\mathrm{SR}}=0.1$, where $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SR}}$ compares the super-resolved image with the high-resolution target warped by the STN transform under stop-gradient, so the reconstruction task can sharpen the image but cannot pull the rectifier toward geometries that are merely easy to reconstruct. Weights are averaged over training \cite{tarvainen2017mean}, and every configuration is run with three seeds on $999$ held-out tracks, where the noise floor is about $\pm 13$ tracks.

Trained this way, MF-SR-OCR reaches $79.95\% \pm 0.15$ over three seeds, improving on the baseline and showing the lowest seed-to-seed variance among the configurations we trained. The controlled ablation that accompanies it, however, does not support the premise on which such architectures are usually justified. Removing the restoration path entirely---the super-resolution branch together with its deformable alignment---while holding every other setting fixed yields $80.45\% \pm 0.45$ at $4.17\times$ fewer floating-point operations per track, a difference that falls inside the validation noise floor and therefore leaves the branch without any measurable benefit to recognition. What does improve recognition, once each factor is held fixed in turn, is the far cheaper combination of geometric rectification, domain-matched degradation, constrained decoding, and weight averaging. A track-level analysis points in the same direction: the plates the model misreads score $2.30$~dB \emph{higher} in PSNR than the ones it reads correctly ($r = -0.41$, $n = 999$), so on this data reconstruction fidelity and legibility are not merely uncorrelated but opposed. We report the negative result together with the positive one.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: Section~2 details the data, the six stages of the proposed architecture, the joint training objective that couples restoration to recognition, the layout-constrained decoder, the reduced configurations used for ablation, and the multi-seed evaluation protocol. Section~3 presents the experimental results, the ablation studies, and the analysis of image-quality metrics against recognition accuracy. Section~4 concludes the paper and outlines directions for future work.

\section{Methodology}

This section describes the proposed MF-SR-OCR framework, beginning with the task and the corpus it is trained on and ending with the protocol behind every number we report. Each component is described together with the reason for its design, since several of the choices below follow from failure modes observed during development rather than from convention.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\subsection{Problem Formulation and Data}

The unit of prediction is a \emph{track}: five consecutive low-resolution captures $\{I^{(1)}_{\mathrm{LR}},\dots,I^{(5)}_{\mathrm{LR}}\}$ of the same plate, for which the model must emit a single seven-character string. A track counts as correct only under exact match, so the metric admits no partial credit. We use the LRLPR benchmark corpus \cite{laroca2026icpr}, whose $20{,}000$ training tracks span two acquisition scenarios and two plate layouts: Scenario-A ($10{,}000$ tracks, PNG, daylight) and the harder Scenario-B ($10{,}000$ tracks, JPEG, including rain and night), each covering the older Brazilian pattern \texttt{LLLNNNN} and the Mercosur pattern \texttt{LLLNLNN}. Every training track additionally provides five high-resolution captures, which supply the targets for the restoration branch.

Validation uses a fixed split of $999$ tracks drawn entirely from Scenario-B, leaving $19{,}001$ tracks for training. Restricting validation to the harder scenario keeps the estimate conservative and, because the split is stored on disk, identical across all runs, so ablations remain comparable. Native low-resolution crops average roughly $46 \times 19$ pixels and fall as low as $38 \times 12$: a seven-character plate is therefore carried by as few as five pixels of width per character, with stroke width of about one pixel. Fig.~\ref{fig2} shows representative tracks at their true sampling grid. All frames are resized to $32 \times 128$ before entering the network, and labels are transcribed over an alphabet of $36$ symbols plus the CTC blank, giving $37$ classes.

\begin{figure}[!h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Methonology/DataSamples.png}
    \caption{Scenario-B tracks at the resolution the network receives, shown without interpolation.}
    \label{fig2}
\end{figure}

Each training track yields two samples. The first uses the real low-resolution frames. The second degrades the high-resolution frames into synthetic low-resolution ones, doubling the effective corpus while preserving the label. Crucially, the degradation is applied \emph{after} the high-resolution image is downscaled to the native low-resolution size, not at high resolution: a blur kernel or JPEG block measured in pixels acts on a $46 \times 19$ image very differently than on a $115 \times 42$ one, and degrading at the wrong scale produces noise statistics that do not match genuine low-resolution captures. We refer to this as domain-matched degradation; its parameters were chosen by matching the Laplacian variance of the synthetic samples to that of real low-resolution crops over $200$ Scenario-B tracks. It is one of the four settings we group as the \emph{baseline cluster} in Section~2.5, and we did not ablate it individually. Real low-resolution samples are additionally augmented with random affine and perspective warps, rotation up to $10^{\circ}$, brightness, contrast and hue jitter, and coarse dropout.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\subsection{Network Architecture}

\begin{figure}[!h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth,  height = 0.4\textheight, keepaspectratio]{Methonology/SystemOverview.png}
    \caption{The MF-SR-OCR pipeline.}
    \label{fig1}
\end{figure}

The network maps a tensor of shape $[B, 5, 3, 32, 128]$ to per-timestep class log-probabilities through the six stages numbered in Fig.~\ref{fig1}. All frames share weights throughout, so the parameter count is independent of the number of frames.

\textbf{Stage 1 --- Per-frame geometric rectification.} A Spatial Transformer Network \cite{jaderberg2015stn} predicts six affine parameters $\theta_i$ per frame and resamples it. The localization network uses stride-1 convolutions with max-pooling and retains a $4 \times 8$ spatial grid before its fully connected head, rather than pooling to $1 \times 1$. This matters because rotation and translation are spatial quantities: a globally averaged descriptor retains little with which to express \emph{``the plate leans five degrees and sits left of centre''}, and a head fed such a descriptor appears able to fit little beyond zoom. The final layer is initialized so that $\theta_i$ is the identity, making the module a no-op at the start of training.

\textbf{Stage 2 --- Cross-frame deformable alignment.} A single affine warp per frame cannot express residual local misalignment caused by motion blur, rolling shutter, or plate bending. We therefore align each frame toward the middle frame of the track with a modulated deformable convolution \cite{zhu2019dcnv2}, following the principle established for video restoration \cite{wang2019edvr}. Offsets and modulation masks are predicted from the concatenated (frame, reference) pair. Both heads are zero-initialized, and---importantly---the deformable kernel itself is initialized to the identity; zeroing only the offsets is insufficient, since a randomly initialized $3\times3$ kernel would replace each RGB frame with a random mixture on the very first forward pass.

\textbf{Stage 3 --- Multi-frame super-resolution.} The restoration branch upscales the aligned frames by $\times 2$. Shallow features are extracted per frame, averaged across the five frames to form a temporal context, and concatenated back onto each frame before a body of four residual blocks; upsampling uses sub-pixel convolution \cite{nascimento2023attention}, and the learned output is added to a bilinear upscale so that the module starts near identity. The temporal context is what makes the branch multi-frame rather than single-image: at five to seven pixels of width per character, the only additional signal plausibly available to it is the sub-pixel jitter between frames.

\textbf{Stage 4 --- Shared residual encoder.} A stem convolution is followed by five residual stages with channel widths $(64, 128, 256, 256, 512)$ and two blocks each, using PReLU activations, squeeze-and-excitation channel re-weighting, and a residual scale of $0.1$ for stability over long schedules. Following the practice of super-resolution networks we omit BatchNorm inside residual blocks, but removing normalization entirely proved untenable once the SR head doubles the spatial size: feature scale drifts and gradients diverge to NaN. We therefore use GroupNorm \cite{wu2018groupnorm}, which normalizes per sample and so avoids the small-batch behaviour of BatchNorm while restoring the stability its removal cost. The stride schedule reduces height to a single row while preserving width as the CTC time axis, yielding $T = 32$ steps: the $128$-pixel input width is doubled by the $\times 2$ branch and then divided by eight. During training, whole frame feature maps are replaced by the track mean with probability $0.05$, discouraging the model from relying on one clear frame.

\textbf{Stage 5 --- Attention fusion.} Frames within a track differ in blur and compression, so they are not equally informative. A small scoring network reduces each frame feature map to a single reliability score; a softmax over the five turns those scores into weights, and the fused map is their weighted sum.

\textbf{Stage 6 --- Sequence head.} The fused map is one row high, so its width axis is read directly as a sequence by a two-layer bidirectional LSTM with $256$ hidden units, followed by LayerNorm, dropout, and a linear projection to the $37$ classes. Training uses the CTC objective \cite{graves2006ctc}, which learns the image-to-string alignment without character segmentation, exactly as in the CRNN recognizer this pipeline extends \cite{shi2017crnn}. The full model has $29.58$M parameters.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\subsection{Joint Training Objective}

The six stages above define what the network computes; the objective below defines what it is trained to optimize. Nothing so far constrains the images leaving Stage~3 to resemble the plate: under the recognition loss alone, that branch could produce any representation the encoder finds convenient. Supervising it against the high-resolution frames is what makes the pipeline jointly trained rather than merely sequential. Restoration and recognition are therefore optimized under a single loss:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Total}} = \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CTC}} + \lambda_{\mathrm{SR}} \cdot \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SR}},
\label{eq:total}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CTC}}$ is the transcription loss over the seven-character label and $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SR}}$ supervises the restoration branch against the high-resolution frames:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SR}} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}
  \left\| I_{\mathrm{SR}}^{(i)} - \mathrm{Warp}_{\mathrm{sg}[\theta_i]}\!\left(I_{\mathrm{HR}}^{(i)}\right) \right\|_1 ,
\label{eq:sr}
\end{equation}
with $N$ the number of frames in the batch that have a high-resolution target and $\lambda_{\mathrm{SR}} = 0.1$ throughout.

Two details of Eq.~\eqref{eq:sr} are deliberate. First, only the \emph{target} is warped. The restoration branch runs after the STN, so $I_{\mathrm{SR}}$ already lives in the rectified frame while $I_{\mathrm{HR}}$ does not; warping both would apply the geometric transform twice, and warping neither would compare two different geometries, leaving the SR head able to learn nothing but blur. Second, the warp uses a stop-gradient copy of $\theta_i$, written $\mathrm{sg}[\cdot]$. Without it, the branch could reduce $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SR}}$ by pulling the STN toward whichever geometry is easiest to reconstruct, letting the auxiliary objective override the primary one. With it, the branch may sharpen the image but cannot steer the rectifier. The implementation also supports a VGG perceptual term and a Sobel edge term inside $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SR}}$; both weights are set to zero in every configuration reported in this paper, so Eq.~\eqref{eq:sr} is exact for all results in Section~3.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\subsection{Layout-Constrained CTC Decoding}

The objective of Section~2.3 governs training; one further choice belongs to inference. The CTC head of Stage~6 is free to emit any symbol at any position, and on this corpus most of that freedom is unnecessary. Every label is seven characters long and follows one of two patterns, \texttt{LLLNNNN} or \texttt{LLLNLNN}. The two differ only at position five, so six of the seven positions have a class---letter or digit---that is fixed regardless of layout. We therefore run a CTC prefix beam search of width $16$ in which a prefix is discarded as soon as it violates both patterns. This is a decode-time constraint only: it adds no parameters, requires no retraining, and leaves the training objective untouched.

The \emph{w/o~SR} variant drops the second term of Eq.~\eqref{eq:total} together with the modules that produce it: the restoration branch, the deformable alignment, and the temporal averaging inside that branch. It therefore isolates the contribution of the restoration path as a whole. The attention fusion across frames is retained, so this variant is still a multi-frame recognizer. The \emph{SR$\times 1$} variant keeps the restoration path but sets the upscaling factor to one, recovering the same $T = 32$ timesteps as the full model through a denser width schedule instead. The pair separates two effects that a naive comparison confounds: doubling the input width also doubles the CTC sequence length, so a gain attributed to super-resolution could equally come from having twice as many timesteps per character. The run identifiers in the last column of Table~\ref{tab:configs} are the internal names under which each configuration was trained and logged; they are given only so that the released logs can be matched to the table.

\subsection{Optimization and Evaluation Protocol}

Models are trained with AdamW at a peak learning rate of $8\times10^{-4}$ and weight decay $10^{-4}$, using a cosine schedule with $5\%$ linear warmup decaying to $5\%$ of the peak, gradients clipped at global norm $2.0$, and mixed precision. The effective batch size is $64$ (micro-batch $32$ with two accumulation steps). Runs last at most $60$ epochs with early stopping after $18$ epochs without improvement. Selection is by validation exact-match accuracy rather than validation loss, since the two diverge in practice: validation loss reaches its minimum around epoch $16$--$20$ and then rises, while exact-match accuracy keeps improving until epoch $24$--$32$. An exponential moving average of the weights with decay $0.999$ \cite{tarvainen2017mean} is maintained throughout, and the averaged weights are what we evaluate and report.

Because the differences separating LRLPR variants are small, single-run comparisons carry little information. On $999$ validation tracks one track is worth $0.1$ percentage points, and the binomial standard deviation of a single measurement near $80\%$ accuracy is about $13$ tracks; a one-point gap between two single runs is therefore well inside what chance alone produces. Every configuration is instead trained with three seeds ($42$, $100$, $2026$) and reported as mean $\pm$ standard deviation, with cuDNN autotuning disabled so that a run can be repeated exactly. We call a difference between two configurations meaningful only when it exceeds twice the standard error of that difference.

Beyond exact-match accuracy we report character error rate and normalized edit distance, which separate configurations whose exact-match scores are close, and---for configurations containing the restoration branch---PSNR and SSIM of the super-resolved frames against the high-resolution ground truth. Because image-quality scores on this corpus are difficult to interpret in absolute terms \cite{nascimento2025toward}, we always report the latter two alongside the bilinear upscale of the same input and read only the difference between them.

Two caveats follow from this protocol. Early stopping selects the reported checkpoint on the same validation split the results are then measured on, so the absolute numbers are mildly optimistic; the comparisons between configurations remain sound, because every configuration is selected and measured in the same way on the same tracks. And every number in this paper is a validation number. The benchmark distributes its public and blind test sets without labels, so no test accuracy can be computed locally: we produced predictions for all $1{,}000$ public and $3{,}000$ blind tracks from a single checkpoint---the seed-$42$ run of the full model, selected because its score is invariant to cuDNN autotuning---but did not submit them, and no test-set result is claimed anywhere in this paper. That checkpoint is also the weakest of the three seeds on validation, so the choice cannot be read as picking the most favourable run.

The nine runs behind Section~3---three configurations at three seeds---cost $39.1$ GPU-hours in total on a single GPU, distributed as $5.6$~h for \emph{w/o~SR}, $22.2$~h for the full model and $11.3$~h for \emph{SR$\times 1$}. The restoration path accounts for most of this difference: it makes the proposed model $4.01\times$ more expensive to train, $4.17\times$ more expensive in floating-point operations per track, and $3.76\times$ slower at inference ($192.8$ against $51.3$~ms per track) than the variant without it.

\section{Results and Discussion}

This section first places the three configurations against the baselines published with the benchmark, then examines whether the restoration branch justifies its cost. That examination returns a negative answer, and the two subsections after it trace the reason: first through the image-quality metrics that motivated the branch, then through the errors the model actually makes.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\subsection{Main Comparison}

The full model reaches $79.95\% \pm 0.15$, about three points above the multi-frame CRNN+STN baseline it extends and above the stronger ResNet+Transformer+STN baseline as well. Its three seeds span $0.30$ points, against $0.90$ for the variant without restoration and $0.80$ for the one without upscaling, so it is also the most stable configuration we trained.

The baseline rows carry a caveat. They are the numbers distributed with the competition's baseline material\footnote{Baseline implementation and reported accuracies: \url{https://github.com/duongtruongbinh/MultiFrame-LPR}}, measured on the organizers' split rather than on ours, so the comparison is indicative rather than exact. Our own reproduction of CRNN+STN on the split used here scored about $75.8\%$, which would place the improvement closer to four points than to three.

Among our three configurations the comparison is more restrained. Under the criterion of Section~2.6---a gap counts only when it exceeds twice the standard error of the difference---one of the three pairwise comparisons survives. The variant without upscaling is worse than the variant without restoration by $0.97$ points against a standard error of $0.36$. The two comparisons involving the full model, $+0.50 \pm 0.28$ against \emph{w/o~SR} and $+0.47 \pm 0.27$ against \emph{SR$\times 1$}, both fall inside the noise. On this data the full model and the variant that drops restoration entirely are statistically indistinguishable.

The character error rates in Table~\ref{tab:main} order the three configurations the same way as exact match, but they are not simply a restatement of it. The variant without restoration is best on both, and it is also the only configuration that never emitted a prediction of the wrong length: across its three seeds it produced $0$, $0$ and $0$ length errors, against $0$, $1$, $0$ for the full model and $1$, $0$, $0$ for \emph{SR$\times 1$}. The two metrics do, however, disagree about stability. \emph{SR$\times 1$} has by far the steadiest character error rate, $\pm 0.0001$ against $\pm 0.0016$ for the full model, while having the least steady exact-match rate, $\pm 0.44$ against $\pm 0.15$. A configuration can therefore be consistent about how many characters it gets wrong and inconsistent about which tracks it gets entirely right, which is worth keeping in mind when a single metric is used to rank near-tied models.

Layout-constrained decoding adds little on top of this. Disabling it changes $0$, $2$ and $0$ tracks across the three seeds. It costs nothing at inference and did not degrade a prediction in any of our runs, so we retain it, but it does not appear to be the source of the accuracy.

\subsection{Does the Restoration Branch Justify Its Cost?}

The branch does learn something. Fig.~\ref{fig:srloss} tracks its reconstruction loss against a bilinear upscale of the same input. The learned path stays below interpolation at every one of the $46$ epochs, and the margin widens from $2.5\%$ at the first epoch to $13.8\%$ at convergence, so the branch is not simply reproducing what interpolation already supplies.

\begin{figure}[!h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Result/SRLossVsBilinear.png}
    \caption{Reconstruction loss of the learned branch against a bilinear upscale of the same input: (a) both losses per epoch, (b) their relative gap.}
    \label{fig:srloss}
\end{figure}

That improvement does not appear to reach recognition. Removing the branch, its deformable alignment and the temporal averaging inside it---while holding the backbone, the schedule and the baseline cluster fixed---moves accuracy from $79.95\%$ to $80.45\%$, a change inside the noise floor, and reduces computation from $109.08$ to $26.14$ GFLOPs per track, a factor of $4.17$. At track level the reduced variant is ahead at every seed, by $2$, $8$ and $5$ tracks; the direction is consistent but the magnitude is not distinguishable from chance. Training cost follows the same ratio, $22.2$ GPU-hours for the full model against $5.6$ for the variant without it.

Two readings of this result remain open. The first is that restoration does not help this task; the second is that our particular branch is too weak and a stronger one would. Our data does not separate them---it establishes only that this branch, at this cost, buys nothing measurable. The large multi-frame gains reported elsewhere \cite{na2025mflpr2,nascimento2025toward} were obtained on different corpora and with heavier restoration front-ends, so they neither contradict nor settle the question for this benchmark. What it does not support is the explanation we assumed at the outset, namely that the improvement of the full pipeline came from super-resolution. Since the configuration without any restoration matches it, the improvement over the baseline appears to rest on the four settings shared by all three configurations: the $4 \times 8$ localization grid, domain-matched degradation, constrained decoding and weight averaging. Decomposing that cluster is the natural next experiment and is left for future work.

The upscaling factor gives a clearer result. \emph{SR$\times 1$} retains the branch but removes the $\times 2$ upscale, recovering the same $32$ CTC timesteps through a denser width schedule instead, and falls $0.97$ points behind \emph{w/o~SR}---the one difference in Table~\ref{tab:main} that clears the significance threshold. Doubling the input width thus appears not to be a costless way of obtaining sequence length.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\subsection{Reconstruction Fidelity Is Not Recognition Performance}

Prior work on plate super-resolution reports that PSNR and SSIM track recognition accuracy poorly \cite{nascimento2025toward,tsai2025deep}; our measurements go one step further, in that the two quantities move in opposite directions here. Measured in the conventional way, the branch succeeds. Against the high-resolution targets it gains $+0.72$~dB PSNR and $+0.043$ SSIM over the bilinear baseline, and \emph{SR$\times 1$} gains more still, $+1.04$~dB and $+0.076$. \emph{SR$\times 1$} is nonetheless the weakest recognizer of the three.

Fig.~\ref{fig:psnr} makes the inversion explicit at track level. Panel~(a) separates the $999$ validation tracks by whether the model read them correctly: the misread tracks average $18.42$~dB against $16.12$~dB for the correct ones, so the failures score $2.30$~dB \emph{higher}, and the point-biserial correlation between PSNR and correctness is $r = -0.41$. Panel~(b) groups the same tracks into PSNR quintiles, where exact-match accuracy falls monotonically from $93.5\%$ in the lowest quintile to $51.0\%$ in the highest. Repeating the measurement on \emph{SR$\times 1$}---a different checkpoint, and one whose output resolution differs---gives $r = -0.40$ with a gap of $2.22$~dB, which is consistent with the effect belonging to the data rather than to a single run.

\begin{figure}[!h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Result/PsnrVsCorrect.png}
    \caption{PSNR against readability on the $999$ validation tracks: (a) the two distributions, (b) exact-match accuracy per PSNR quintile.}
    \label{fig:psnr}
\end{figure}

A plausible explanation is that PSNR here is dominated by image content rather than by restoration quality. A blurred, low-contrast plate has little high-frequency detail to lose, so any reconstruction of it scores well while remaining unreadable; a sharp plate carries exactly the fine detail that is hard to reconstruct and easy to read. On this reading PSNR would be tracking how easily an image can be rebuilt, which on this data appears close to the opposite of how much readable evidence it contains. The practical implication is narrower than a claim about image-quality metrics in general: on this data, selecting or supervising a model by PSNR alone would risk moving it away from legibility rather than towards it. This is a concrete argument for the coupling in Eq.~\eqref{eq:total}, where the reconstruction term is subordinate to a recognition loss rather than standing alone as a pixel criterion.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\subsection{Error Analysis}

Of the $202$ tracks the full model gets wrong at seed $42$, $114$---$56.4\%$---differ from the ground truth in exactly one character, and a further $18.3\%$ in two. This accounts for the distance between the two metrics in Table~\ref{tab:main}: a character error rate of $0.054$ and an exact-match rate of $79.95\%$ describe the same predictions, one counting characters and the other refusing partial credit. It also indicates where the remaining headroom lies, since most failures are single confusions rather than collapses, typically between glyph pairs that survive downsampling poorly.

Confidence does not identify those failures. Of the $202$, $103$ carry a decoder confidence above $0.9$, and the mean confidence over all failures is $0.868$. A confidence threshold intended to catch errors would therefore miss slightly more than half of them, which is a caution for any deployment that plans to gate on the model's own certainty. It also matters for the benchmark itself, whose tie-break criterion is the confidence gap between correct and incorrect predictions: over three seeds the full model separates the two groups by only $0.124 \pm 0.018$ ($0.990$ against $0.868$ at seed $42$), so a ranking decided on that margin would rest on a quantity the model estimates poorly.

About a fifth of the validation set is seed-dependent. Comparing the three seeds of each configuration track by track, the predictions agree on $789$ of $999$ tracks for \emph{w/o~SR}, $778$ for the full model and $768$ for \emph{SR$\times 1$}. Each configuration therefore changes $21$--$23\%$ of its answers between otherwise identical runs, which is the strongest support we can offer for the multi-seed protocol of Section~2.6.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\subsection{Limitations}

Four limits bound what we can claim. All results are validation results on $999$ Scenario-B tracks; we produced predictions for the official test splits but never scored them, so nothing reported here can be compared against the competition leaderboard. The baseline cluster was never decomposed, so we can state that the improvement over the baseline does not come from super-resolution, but not yet which of the four remaining settings it does come from. The backbone is identical in all three configurations, so this paper offers no evidence about it in either direction. And we compare against the baselines shipped with the benchmark rather than against a modern scene-text recognizer such as PARSeq \cite{bautista2022parseq} or SVTR \cite{du2022svtr}; that comparison is the most useful single addition we can identify to this work.

\section{Conclusion}

We presented MF-SR-OCR, a multi-frame framework in which the restoration and the recognition of low-resolution license plates are trained under a single objective, with the reconstruction term supervised through a stop-gradient warp so that it can sharpen the image without steering the geometric rectifier. Over three seeds on $999$ authentic surveillance tracks the model reaches $79.95\% \pm 0.15$, above the multi-frame CRNN+STN baseline it extends and with the lowest seed-to-seed variance of the configurations we trained. A controlled ablation nonetheless removes the restoration path entirely and matches that accuracy at $4.17\times$ fewer floating-point operations, so on this benchmark the branch shows no measurable recognition gain---despite reconstructing more faithfully than bilinear interpolation at every epoch of training.

Two observations follow from that result. Reconstruction fidelity and readability are not merely uncorrelated on this data but opposed: the tracks the model misreads score $2.30$~dB higher in PSNR than the ones it reads correctly, and exact-match accuracy falls monotonically across PSNR quintiles. And the margins separating architectural variants here are no larger than the variation between random seeds, which leaves single-run comparison uninformative. Taken together, the improvement over the baseline appears to rest on geometric rectification, domain-matched degradation, constrained decoding and weight averaging rather than on super-resolution. Decomposing that cluster, and comparing against a modern scene-text recognizer such as PARSeq \cite{bautista2022parseq}, are the two experiments we would run next.

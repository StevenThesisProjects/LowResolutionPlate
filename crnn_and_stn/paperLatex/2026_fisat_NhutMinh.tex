\begin{abstract}
Low-Resolution License Plate Recognition (LRLPR) remains a critical challenge in surveillance due to severe motion blur, long capture distances, and lossy video compression. While traditional pipelines treat image restoration and character recognition as decoupled stages, this work investigates the synergy of end-to-end joint optimization. We introduce MF-SR-OCR, a unified Multi-Frame Super-Resolution and Recognition framework tailored for low-resolution video tracks. The proposed architecture integrates per-frame Spatial Transformer Network (STN) alignment, deformable (DCNv2) cross-frame feature alignment, pixel-supervised super-resolution, attention-guided multi-frame fusion, and layout-constrained Connectionist Temporal Classification (CTC) decoding, optimized jointly using Exponential Moving Average (EMA) weight smoothing. Systematic ablation isolates the contributions of a GroupNorm-stabilized ResBlock backbone and pixel-level restoration against a multi-frame CRNN+STN baseline. Evaluated on authentic surveillance tracks, MF-SR-OCR consistently outperforms all baseline variants beyond the validation noise floor. Crucially, the learned super-resolution branch maintains a reconstruction loss significantly below bilinear interpolation baselines throughout training, demonstrating genuine sub-pixel detail recovery rather than cosmetic smoothing. Our findings validate joint restoration-recognition optimization for unconstrained LRLPR and highlight the critical necessity of multi-seed evaluation when benchmarking subtle architectural iterations.
\keywords{license plate recognition \and low-resolution image \and super resolution \and spatial transformer network \and multi-frame fusion.}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Automatic License Plate Recognition (ALPR) is close to solved on sharp images: convolutional-recurrent recognizers transcribe a cropped plate end to end without character segmentation \cite{shi2017crnn}, provided the test images resemble the training ones \cite{laroca2022cross}. Surveillance footage breaks that condition. Long capture distances, low sensor resolution, motion blur, and video compression shrink character strokes to a few pixels, and accuracy on genuinely low-resolution plates falls by more than an order of magnitude \cite{nascimento2025toward}. A plate also counts as correct only when all seven characters are right, so one confused glyph invalidates the whole track. Low-Resolution License Plate Recognition (LRLPR) is therefore treated as a separate problem, formalized by a recent benchmark of $20{,}000$ tracks that pair five low-resolution with five high-resolution captures of the same plate under real, not synthetic, degradation; its winning entry reaches only $82.13\%$ \cite{laroca2026icpr}.

The usual remedy is a super-resolution (SR) stage placed before the recognizer. Plate-specific models improve on generic restorers using attention with sub-pixel upsampling \cite{nascimento2023attention}, layout and character priors \cite{nascimento2024lcdnet}, or embedding-level consistency between high-resolution and super-resolved plates \cite{sendjasni2025embedding}. A second line exploits temporal redundancy, since noise and sub-pixel offsets differ across the frames of a track. Deformable alignment established this idea for video restoration \cite{wang2019edvr}, and multi-frame methods report the largest gains for plates: from $31.1\%$ to $44.7\%$ by voting over five super-resolved frames \cite{nascimento2025toward}, and from $14.04\%$ to $86.44\%$ with optical-flow-based restoration \cite{na2025mflpr2}.

Three gaps remain. First, restoration and recognition are trained separately: the SR module minimizes a pixel loss and passes its output to a frozen recognizer, so it never sees the recognition error. Second, the metrics used to supervise that module predict OCR accuracy poorly, as reported for license plates \cite{tsai2025deep,nascimento2025toward}, and generative restorers can invent plausible but wrong characters \cite{na2025mflpr2}. Third, LRLPR variants are usually compared with single runs, even though the gaps between them are as small as the variation between seeds.

We address these gaps with MF-SR-OCR, built on the benchmark's multi-frame CRNN+STN baseline ($77.00\%$). Five low-resolution frames are rectified per frame by a Spatial Transformer Network \cite{jaderberg2015stn}, aligned across frames by identity-initialized DCNv2 kernels \cite{zhu2019dcnv2}, super-resolved $\times 2$, fused by attention over a GroupNorm residual backbone \cite{wu2018groupnorm}, and decoded by a BiLSTM head under CTC \cite{graves2006ctc} constrained to the two plate layouts in the corpus. Training uses one objective, $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CTC}} + \lambda_{\mathrm{SR}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SR}}$ with $\lambda_{\mathrm{SR}}=0.1$, where $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SR}}$ compares the super-resolved image with the high-resolution target warped by the STN transform under stop-gradient, so the reconstruction task can sharpen the image but cannot pull the rectifier toward geometries that are merely easy to reconstruct. Weights are averaged over training \cite{tarvainen2017mean}, and every configuration is run with three seeds on $999$ held-out tracks, where the noise floor is about $\pm 13$ tracks.

We make three contributions. (i) A jointly optimized multi-frame restoration-and-recognition model reaching $79.95\% \pm 0.15$, above the $77.00\%$ baseline and the most stable configuration we tested. (ii) A controlled negative result: removing the restoration path---super-resolution and its deformable alignment---with all other settings fixed, gives $80.45\% \pm 0.45$ at $3.76\times$ less compute---a statistical tie, so the SR branch shows no measurable benefit, and the gain over the baseline comes from rectification, domain-matched training, constrained decoding, and weight averaging. (iii) Evidence that reconstruction quality opposes legibility: misread tracks score $2.30$~dB \emph{higher} in PSNR than correct ones ($r = -0.41$, $n = 999$). Section~2 describes the architecture and objective, Section~3 the experiments, and Section~4 concludes.

\section{Methodology}

This section describes the proposed MF-SR-OCR framework. We first state the task and the data it is trained on, then detail the five stages of the network, the joint training objective that couples restoration to recognition, the layout-constrained decoder, and finally the optimization and evaluation protocol used for every reported number.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\subsection{Problem Formulation and Data}

The unit of prediction is a \emph{track}: five consecutive low-resolution captures $\{I^{(1)}_{\mathrm{LR}},\dots,I^{(5)}_{\mathrm{LR}}\}$ of the same plate, for which the model must emit a single seven-character string. A track counts as correct only under exact match, so the metric admits no partial credit. We use the LRLPR benchmark corpus \cite{laroca2026icpr}, whose $20{,}000$ training tracks span two acquisition scenarios and two plate layouts: Scenario-A ($10{,}000$ tracks, PNG, daylight) and the harder Scenario-B ($10{,}000$ tracks, JPEG, including rain and night), each covering the older Brazilian pattern \texttt{LLLNNNN} and the Mercosur pattern \texttt{LLLNLNN}. Every training track additionally provides five high-resolution captures, which supply the targets for the restoration branch.

Validation uses a fixed split of $999$ tracks drawn entirely from Scenario-B, leaving $19{,}001$ tracks for training. Restricting validation to the harder scenario keeps the estimate conservative and, because the split is stored on disk, identical across all runs, so ablations remain comparable. Native low-resolution crops average roughly $46 \times 19$ pixels and fall as low as $38 \times 12$: a seven-character plate is therefore carried by as few as five pixels of width per character, with stroke width of about one pixel. Fig.~\ref{fig2} shows representative tracks at their true sampling grid. All frames are resized to $32 \times 128$ before entering the network, and labels are transcribed over an alphabet of $36$ symbols plus the CTC blank, giving $37$ classes.

\begin{figure}[!h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Methonology/DataSamples.png}
    \caption{Representative Scenario-B tracks shown without interpolation, at the resolution the network receives. Five consecutive low-resolution frames of the same plate (left) differ in blur, compression and sub-pixel offset---the redundancy the multi-frame branches exploit---while the high-resolution capture (right) supplies the target for the restoration branch.}
    \label{fig2}
\end{figure}

Each training track yields two samples. The first uses the real low-resolution frames. The second degrades the high-resolution frames into synthetic low-resolution ones, doubling the effective corpus while preserving the label. Crucially, the degradation is applied \emph{after} the high-resolution image is downscaled to the native low-resolution size, not at high resolution: a blur kernel or JPEG block measured in pixels acts on a $46 \times 19$ image very differently than on a $115 \times 42$ one, and degrading at the wrong scale produces noise statistics that do not match genuine low-resolution captures. We refer to this as domain-matched degradation; its parameters were chosen by matching the Laplacian variance of the synthetic samples to that of real low-resolution crops over $200$ Scenario-B tracks. It is one of four settings we group as the \emph{baseline cluster} in Section~3, and we did not ablate it individually. Real low-resolution samples are additionally augmented with random affine and perspective warps, rotation up to $10^{\circ}$, brightness, contrast and hue jitter, and coarse dropout.

\subsection{Network Architecture}

\begin{figure}[!h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth,  height = 0.4\textheight, keepaspectratio]{Methonology/SystemOverview.png}
    \caption{The MF-SR-OCR pipeline. Five low-resolution frames are rectified per frame (STN), aligned across frames (DCNv2), super-resolved jointly, encoded by a shared residual backbone, fused by attention, and decoded by a BiLSTM under layout-constrained CTC. The super-resolution branch is supervised by the high-resolution frames through a stop-gradient warp.}
    \label{fig1}
\end{figure}

The network maps a tensor of shape $[B, 5, 3, 32, 128]$ to per-timestep class log-probabilities through five stages, illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig1}. All frames share weights throughout, so the parameter count is independent of the number of frames.

\textbf{Stage 1 --- Per-frame geometric rectification.} A Spatial Transformer Network \cite{jaderberg2015stn} predicts six affine parameters $\theta_i$ per frame and resamples it. The localization network uses stride-1 convolutions with max-pooling and retains a $4 \times 8$ spatial grid before its fully connected head, rather than pooling to $1 \times 1$. This matters because rotation and translation are spatial quantities: a globally averaged descriptor cannot express \emph{``the plate leans five degrees and sits left of centre''}, and a head fed such a descriptor can fit little beyond zoom. The final layer is initialized so that $\theta_i$ is the identity, making the module a no-op at the start of training.

\textbf{Stage 2 --- Cross-frame deformable alignment.} A single affine warp per frame cannot express residual local misalignment caused by motion blur, rolling shutter, or plate bending. We therefore align each frame toward the middle frame of the track with a modulated deformable convolution \cite{zhu2019dcnv2}, following the principle established for video restoration \cite{wang2019edvr}. Offsets and modulation masks are predicted from the concatenated (frame, reference) pair. Both heads are zero-initialized, and---importantly---the deformable kernel itself is initialized to the identity; zeroing only the offsets is insufficient, since a randomly initialized $3\times3$ kernel would replace each RGB frame with a random mixture on the very first forward pass.

\textbf{Stage 3 --- Multi-frame super-resolution.} The restoration branch upscales the aligned frames by $\times 2$. Shallow features are extracted per frame, averaged across the five frames to form a temporal context, and concatenated back onto each frame before a body of four residual blocks; upsampling uses sub-pixel convolution \cite{nascimento2023attention}, and the learned output is added on top of a bilinear upscale so the module also starts near identity. The temporal context is the reason the branch is multi-frame rather than single-image: at five to seven pixels of width per character a single-image restorer cannot add information it never received---it can only hallucinate, and hallucination is indistinguishable from a reading error. Frames carrying independent sub-pixel jitter do supply genuine additional signal, and this is where it enters.

\textbf{Stage 4 --- Shared residual encoder.} A stem convolution is followed by five residual stages with channel widths $(64, 128, 256, 256, 512)$ and two blocks each, using PReLU activations, squeeze-and-excitation channel re-weighting, and a residual scale of $0.1$ for stability over long schedules. Following the practice of super-resolution networks we omit BatchNorm inside residual blocks, but removing normalization entirely proved untenable once the SR head doubles the spatial size: feature scale drifts and gradients diverge to NaN. We therefore use GroupNorm \cite{wu2018groupnorm}, which normalizes per sample and so avoids the small-batch behaviour of BatchNorm while restoring the stability its removal cost. The stride schedule reduces height to a single row while preserving width as the CTC time axis, yielding $T = 32$ steps: the $128$-pixel input width is doubled by the $\times 2$ branch and then divided by eight. During training, whole frame feature maps are replaced by the track mean with probability $0.05$, discouraging the model from relying on one clear frame.

\textbf{Stage 5 --- Fusion and sequence decoding} (blocks 5 and 6 in Fig.~\ref{fig1})\textbf{.} A scoring network reduces each frame feature map to a scalar reliability score; a softmax over frames produces weights, and the fused map is their weighted sum. Because the fused map has unit height, its width axis is read directly as a sequence by a two-layer bidirectional LSTM with $256$ hidden units, followed by LayerNorm, dropout, and a linear projection to the $37$ classes. Training uses the CTC objective \cite{graves2006ctc}, which learns the image-to-string alignment without character segmentation, as in the CRNN recognizer this pipeline extends \cite{shi2017crnn}. The full model has $29.58$M parameters.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\subsection{Joint Training Objective}

Restoration and recognition are optimized under a single loss:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Total}} = \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CTC}} + \lambda_{\mathrm{SR}} \cdot \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SR}},
\label{eq:total}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CTC}}$ is the transcription loss over the seven-character label and $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SR}}$ supervises the restoration branch against the high-resolution frames:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SR}} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}
  \left\| I_{\mathrm{SR}}^{(i)} - \mathrm{Warp}_{\mathrm{sg}[\theta_i]}\!\left(I_{\mathrm{HR}}^{(i)}\right) \right\|_1 ,
\label{eq:sr}
\end{equation}
with $N$ the number of frames in the batch that have a high-resolution target and $\lambda_{\mathrm{SR}} = 0.1$ throughout.

Two details of Eq.~\eqref{eq:sr} are deliberate. First, only the \emph{target} is warped. The restoration branch runs after the STN, so $I_{\mathrm{SR}}$ already lives in the rectified frame while $I_{\mathrm{HR}}$ does not; warping both would apply the geometric transform twice, and warping neither would compare two different geometries, leaving the SR head able to learn nothing but blur. Second, the warp uses a stop-gradient copy of $\theta_i$, written $\mathrm{sg}[\cdot]$. Without it, the branch could reduce $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SR}}$ by pulling the STN toward whichever geometry is easiest to reconstruct, letting the auxiliary objective override the primary one. With it, the branch may sharpen the image but cannot steer the rectifier. The implementation also supports a VGG perceptual term and a Sobel edge term inside $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SR}}$; both weights are set to zero in every configuration reported in this paper, so Eq.~\eqref{eq:sr} is exact for all results in Section~3.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\subsection{Layout-Constrained CTC Decoding}

Unconstrained CTC decoding may emit any symbol at any position, whereas every label in the corpus is seven characters long and follows one of two patterns, \texttt{LLLNNNN} or \texttt{LLLNLNN}. The two differ only at position five, so six of the seven positions have a class---letter or digit---that is fixed regardless of layout. We therefore run a CTC prefix beam search of width $16$ in which a prefix is discarded as soon as it violates both patterns. This is a decode-time constraint only: it adds no parameters, requires no retraining, and leaves the training objective untouched.

The \emph{w/o~SR} variant removes the restoration branch, the deformable alignment, and the temporal averaging performed inside the restoration branch, isolating the contribution of the whole restoration path; the attention fusion across frames is retained, so this variant is still a multi-frame recognizer. The \emph{SR$\times 1$} variant keeps the restoration path but sets the upscaling factor to one, recovering the same $T = 32$ timesteps as the full model through a denser width schedule instead. The pair separates two effects that a naive comparison confounds: doubling the input width also doubles the CTC sequence length, so a gain attributed to super-resolution could equally come from having twice as many timesteps per character. The run identifiers in the last column are the internal names under which each configuration was trained and logged; they are given only so that the released logs can be matched to the table.

\subsection{Optimization and Evaluation Protocol}

Models are trained with AdamW at a peak learning rate of $8\times10^{-4}$ and weight decay $10^{-4}$, using a cosine schedule with $5\%$ linear warmup decaying to $5\%$ of the peak, gradients clipped at global norm $2.0$, and mixed precision. The effective batch size is $64$ (micro-batch $32$ with two accumulation steps). Runs last at most $60$ epochs with early stopping after $18$ epochs without improvement. Selection is by validation exact-match accuracy rather than validation loss, since the two diverge in practice: validation loss reaches its minimum around epoch $16$--$20$ and then rises, while exact-match accuracy keeps improving until epoch $24$--$32$. An exponential moving average of the weights with decay $0.999$ \cite{tarvainen2017mean} is maintained throughout, and the averaged weights are what we evaluate and report.

Because the differences separating LRLPR variants are small, single-run comparisons are not informative. On $999$ validation tracks, one track is $0.1$ percentage points and the binomial standard deviation near $80\%$ accuracy is approximately $13$ tracks, so any gap below roughly $1.3$ points is indistinguishable from chance. Every configuration is therefore trained with three seeds ($42$, $100$, $2026$) and reported as mean $\pm$ standard deviation, with cuDNN autotuning disabled so that runs are reproducible. We consider a difference between two configurations meaningful only when it exceeds twice the standard error of that difference. Beyond exact-match accuracy we report character error rate and normalized edit distance, which separate configurations whose exact-match scores are close, and---for configurations containing the restoration branch---PSNR and SSIM of the super-resolved frames against the high-resolution ground truth, always alongside the bilinear upscale of the same input, since only the difference between the two is interpretable \cite{nascimento2025toward}. All numbers we report are validation numbers: the benchmark's held-out test sets are unlabelled, and we did not submit to them, so no test-set result is claimed anywhere in this paper.

\section{Numerical Results and Discussion}


To ensure experimental consistency and reproducibility, a unified hyperparameter configuration was enforced across all candidate architectures. The models were implemented within the TensorFlow deep learning framework. Network weights were optimized using the Adam algorithm with a static learning rate of $\alpha = 5 \times 10^{-4}$. This specific learning rate was empirically selected to balance convergence speed with stability, effectively mitigating the gradient volatility often observed in the recurrent units of the CLDNN and the multi-head attention blocks of the TNN. The loss calculation utilized Sparse Categorical Cross-Entropy, minimizing the divergence between the predicted probability distribution and the ground-truth modulation labels. Further, the performance analysis assesses classification accuracy across a wide dynamic range of signal-to-noise ratios (SNRs), i.e., from $-20$ dB to $+20$ dB.

    \centering

    \caption{Accuracy vs. SNR of different techniques.}
    \label{fig2}

Fig. \ref{fig2} delineates the definitive performance benchmark, wherein the heterogeneous fusion of the Shallow ResNet and TNN (solid red trajectory) establishes a dominant operational envelope across the full dynamic range, peaking at an asymptotic accuracy of $\approx 94\%$ in the high-SNR regime ($> 10\,\text{dB}$) while demonstrating superior gradient stability in the critical transition zone ($0\text{--}4\,\text{dB}$). This empirical success validates the hypothesis of feature orthogonality: the architecture effectively synergizes the high-precision spatial filtering of the ResNet—essential for resolving dense, static constellations (e.g., 64-QAM)—with the global, noise-resilient context of the TNN, thereby rectifying errors in noise-dominated samples where local convolutional filters lose discriminative power and yielding a calibrated, robust classification output. In stark contrast, the CLDNN-TNN configuration yields suboptimal inference results (saturating at $90\%\text{--}91\%$) due to feature redundancy and overlapping inductive biases; since both constituent architectures (via recurrent units and self-attention, respectively) are predisposed toward extracting temporal dependencies, they exhibit correlated error manifolds—particularly in differentiating high-order memoryless modulations like 64-QAM vs. 8-PSK—resulting in a compounded "temporal bias" that fails to provide the requisite diversity needed for effective ensembling.

The confusion matrix presented in Fig. \ref{fig3} delineates the system's performance within the critical "Transition Regime" ($\text{SNR} \approx 0\,\text{dB}$), where the ensemble demonstrates a strategic fusion of spatial rigidity and contextual resilience. Notably, the architecture achieves exceptional fidelity in analog domains—recording $93\%$ accuracy for Wideband Frequency Modulation (WBFM) and exceeding $73\%$ for Amplitude Modulation schemes—a result we attribute to the Transformer's global attention mechanism acting as a "spectral validator" that filters spurious local correlations and eliminates the false-positive mode collapse frequently observed in standalone convolutional baselines. While discrimination in dense digital constellations (e.g., QAM, PSK) is naturally bounded between $48\%$ and $60\%$ by the noise-obscured Euclidean distances, the ensemble maintains strict Manifold Preservation: errors are not stochastic but are statistically clustered among spectrally adjacent sub-classes (e.g., misclassifying 64-QAM as 16-QAM). This structured error distribution confirms that the heterogeneous fusion preserves the topological integrity of the modulation space, preventing catastrophic classification bias even when the noise floor challenges definitive symbol discrimination.

To isolate the specific contribution of deep residual spatial filtering, we evaluate the CLDNN-Transformer ensemble, where the performance dynamics reveal a stark divergence contingent on modulation topology. While the classification of continuous analog waveforms remains robust—virtually invariant to the backbone choice due to the Transformer's dominance in resolving global temporal envelopes (matching the 93\% WBFM benchmark of the ResNet-based system)—the digital domain suffers a precipitous degradation in fidelity. Specifically, 16-QAM detection collapses to 39\% (a 9\% deficit relative to the ResNet-TNN benchmark), with concomitant losses across phase-dependent schemes like 8PSK and QPSK. We attribute this sub-optimal performance to feature redundancy and a compound spatial deficit: the CLDNN’s reliance on recurrent aggregation functionally overlaps with the Transformer’s self-attention mechanism, resulting in an ensemble with correlated temporal inductive biases but insufficient spatial resolution. Lacking the deep residual regularization provided by the ResNet to disentangle dense Euclidean clusters, the framework fails to enforce the precise decision boundaries required for high-order memoryless modulations, confirming that rigorous spatial feature extraction is a non-negotiable prerequisite for robust digital signal separation.

\section{Conclusion}

This study advances AMC robustness in stochastic NLOS environments by proposing an SNR-adaptive heterogeneous ensemble that strategically fuses Shallow ResNet spatial filtering with Transformer-based global attention. The primary contribution lies in the exploitation of feature orthogonality to stabilize the decision boundary: the framework leverages ResNet precision to resolve dense constellations in signal-dominated regimes, while uniquely deploying the Transformer as a spectral validator to prevent mode collapse in noise-dominated zones, thereby achieving $\approx 94\%$ asymptotic accuracy. By empirically invalidating redundant spatiotemporal combinations (e.g., CLDNN-TNN), this work confirms that strict architectural diversity is the prerequisite for effective ensembling, establishing a validated foundation for future edge-native, Over-The-Air implementations.

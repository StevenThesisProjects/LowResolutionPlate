\begin{abstract}
Low-Resolution License Plate Recognition (LRLPR) remains a critical challenge in surveillance due to severe motion blur, long capture distances, and lossy video compression. While traditional pipelines treat image restoration and character recognition as decoupled stages, this work investigates the synergy of end-to-end joint optimization. We introduce MF-SR-OCR, a unified Multi-Frame Super-Resolution and Recognition framework tailored for low-resolution video tracks. The proposed architecture integrates per-frame Spatial Transformer Network (STN) alignment, deformable (DCNv2) cross-frame feature alignment, pixel-supervised super-resolution, attention-guided multi-frame fusion, and layout-constrained Connectionist Temporal Classification (CTC) decoding, all trained under a single objective and stabilized by Exponential Moving Average (EMA) weight smoothing. Systematic ablation isolates the contribution of the restoration path itself, and of its upscaling factor, against a multi-frame CRNN+STN baseline. Evaluated across multiple seeds on the authentic, non-synthetic tracks of a recent LRLPR benchmark, MF-SR-OCR improves on that baseline and is the most stable configuration we trained. The learned super-resolution branch does maintain a reconstruction loss below bilinear interpolation throughout training; a controlled ablation that removes the restoration path entirely, however, matches this accuracy at a fraction of the computational cost, so the branch yields no measurable recognition gain on this benchmark. Our findings locate the measurable improvement in geometric rectification, domain-matched degradation, constrained decoding and weight averaging rather than in super-resolution, and highlight the critical necessity of multi-seed evaluation when benchmarking subtle architectural iterations.
\keywords{license plate recognition \and low-resolution image \and super resolution \and spatial transformer network \and multi-frame fusion.}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Reading a license plate from a clear photograph is largely a solved problem. Convolutional-recurrent networks turn a cropped plate straight into a string without segmenting the characters first \cite{shi2017crnn}, and recent systems read plates accurately in unconstrained road scenes \cite{ke2023ultrafast}. Those scenes are unconstrained in pose, lighting and background---not in resolution. Text recognition itself has kept moving since then, and CTC-based models remain competitive with encoder-decoder ones \cite{du2025svtrv2}, but every one of them assumes the glyphs are there to be read. Surveillance video does not cooperate. Cameras sit far from the road, sensors are small, vehicles move, and the footage is compressed before anyone looks at it. By the time a plate reaches the recognizer, a character may be five pixels wide. Accuracy on images like these falls by an order of magnitude \cite{nascimento2025toward}, and the scoring rule leaves no room to recover: a plate counts only when all seven characters are right, so one confused glyph loses the whole track. The ICPR 2026 competition made the setting concrete with $20{,}000$ tracks, each pairing five low-resolution and five high-resolution captures of the same plate, degraded in the field rather than in software. Its winning entry reached $82.13\%$ \cite{laroca2026icpr}.

The usual response is to restore the image before reading it. A generic super-resolution model is the wrong tool here, because a plate is text and its legibility lives in a few thin strokes, so recent work has built restorers that know as much: attention with sub-pixel upsampling \cite{nascimento2023attention}, layout and character priors \cite{nascimento2024lcdnet}, and losses that compare embeddings instead of pixels \cite{sendjasni2025embedding}. A second idea uses the fact that surveillance gives us several frames rather than one. Noise and sub-pixel offsets differ from frame to frame, so five frames together carry more than any one of them. Deformable alignment made this practical for video \cite{wang2019edvr}, and the plate-specific numbers are striking: voting over five super-resolved frames lifts recognition from $31.1\%$ to $44.7\%$ \cite{nascimento2025toward}, and an optical-flow restoration pipeline reports $86.44\%$, against $82.55\%$ for the best multi-frame baseline it compares against and $16.18\%$ for the best single-frame one \cite{na2025mflpr2}.

Three things about this picture still trouble us. First, restoration and recognition are trained apart. The super-resolution module minimizes a pixel loss and hands its output to a recognizer that was never in the loop, so it never learns which details the reader actually needs. Second, the criteria used to supervise it follow recognition accuracy only loosely, a mismatch reported in the plate super-resolution literature \cite{nascimento2025toward} and the reason later work turned to layout- and character-aware objectives \cite{nascimento2024lcdnet,sendjasni2025embedding}. Restorers built on pretrained generative priors carry a risk of their own, having been observed to introduce artifacts and distortions on badly degraded plates \cite{na2025mflpr2}. Third, and more quietly, work in this area tends to compare architectures using one run each. In our experiments the gap between two variants was routinely smaller than the gap between two seeds of the same variant, which makes single-run comparisons hard to read at all.

MF-SR-OCR is our attempt to deal with all three at once. It keeps the multi-frame CRNN+STN baseline shipped with the competition ($77.00\%$) and rebuilds the path in front of it. Five low-resolution frames are first straightened one at a time by a Spatial Transformer Network \cite{jaderberg2015stn}, then aligned to each other by deformable convolutions whose kernels start at the identity \cite{zhu2019dcnv2}, then super-resolved together by a factor of two. A shared residual backbone with GroupNorm \cite{wu2018groupnorm} encodes the result, attention weighs the five frames against one another, and a BiLSTM head decodes under CTC \cite{graves2006ctc} with the two plate layouts of the corpus used as a constraint. Everything trains under one loss, $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CTC}} + \lambda_{\mathrm{SR}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SR}}$ with $\lambda_{\mathrm{SR}}=0.1$. The restoration term compares the super-resolved image against the high-resolution target after warping that target by the same transform the rectifier chose, with the gradient through the transform cut. The branch can therefore sharpen the image, but it cannot quietly steer the rectifier toward whatever geometry is easiest to rebuild. Weights are averaged over training \cite{tarvainen2017mean}, and every configuration is run with three seeds on $999$ held-out tracks, where a difference of about $13$ tracks is what chance alone produces.

Trained this way, MF-SR-OCR reaches $79.95\% \pm 0.15$ across three seeds, above the baseline and with the smallest spread between seeds of anything we trained. The ablation that goes with it is less comfortable. Take the restoration path out entirely, leave every other setting untouched, and accuracy becomes $80.45\% \pm 0.45$ at $4.17\times$ fewer floating-point operations per track. That difference sits inside the noise floor, so the branch buys nothing we can measure. What does help, once each factor is held fixed in turn, is the much cheaper combination of geometric rectification, domain-matched degradation, constrained decoding and weight averaging. A closer look at individual tracks points the same way: the plates the model misreads score $2.30$~dB \emph{higher} in PSNR than the ones it reads correctly ($r = -0.41$, $n = 999$). On this data, reconstruction fidelity and legibility are not simply unrelated; they pull in opposite directions. We report that result alongside the positive one.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section~2 covers the data, the six stages of the architecture, the training objective that ties restoration to recognition, the constrained decoder, the reduced configurations used for ablation, and the evaluation protocol. Section~3 presents the results, the ablations, and the analysis of image-quality metrics against recognition accuracy. Section~4 concludes and points to what we would try next.

\section{Methodology}

This section walks through the framework in the order a track passes through it: first the data and what makes it hard, then the six stages of the network, the loss that ties them together, the decoder, the reduced variants we compare against, and finally the protocol behind every number in Section~3. Where a design choice looks arbitrary, we say what went wrong before we settled on it; several of the details below exist because a simpler version failed.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\subsection{Problem Formulation and Data}

The unit of prediction is a \emph{track}: five consecutive low-resolution captures $\{I^{(1)}_{\mathrm{LR}},\dots,I^{(5)}_{\mathrm{LR}}\}$ of one plate, for which the model returns a single seven-character string. A track is correct only under exact match, so there is no partial credit for getting six characters right. We work with the LRLPR benchmark corpus \cite{laroca2026icpr}. Its $20{,}000$ training tracks come from two acquisition scenarios and two plate layouts: Scenario-A ($10{,}000$ tracks, PNG, daylight) and the harder Scenario-B ($10{,}000$ tracks, JPEG, including rain and night), each covering the older Brazilian pattern \texttt{LLLNNNN} and the Mercosur pattern \texttt{LLLNLNN}. Every training track also carries five high-resolution captures, and those are what the restoration branch is trained against.

For validation we hold out a fixed set of $999$ tracks, all from Scenario-B, leaving $19{,}001$ for training. Drawing them from the harder scenario keeps the estimate conservative, and keeping the split on disk means every ablation is scored on exactly the same tracks. It is worth being concrete about how little signal these images carry. Native crops average roughly $46 \times 19$ pixels and go as low as $38 \times 12$, which leaves about five pixels of width for each of the seven characters and a stroke roughly one pixel thick. Fig.~\ref{fig2} shows a few tracks at their true sampling grid, with no interpolation to flatter them. Everything is resized to $32 \times 128$ on the way in, and labels are written over $36$ symbols plus the CTC blank, giving $37$ classes.

\begin{figure}[!h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Methonology/DataSamples.png}
    \caption{Scenario-B tracks at the resolution the network receives, shown without interpolation.}
    \label{fig2}
\end{figure}

Each training track produces two samples. One uses the real low-resolution frames. The other takes the high-resolution frames and degrades them into synthetic low-resolution ones, which doubles the corpus without touching the labels. The order of operations here matters more than it might seem. We downscale to the native low-resolution size \emph{first} and degrade afterwards, because a blur kernel or a JPEG block is measured in pixels: applied to a $46 \times 19$ image it does something quite different from what it does to a $115 \times 42$ one, and degrading at the wrong scale yields noise that does not resemble a real capture. We call this domain-matched degradation, and we tuned its parameters by matching the Laplacian variance of the synthetic samples against real low-resolution crops over $200$ Scenario-B tracks. It is one of the four settings we later group as the \emph{baseline cluster} (Section~2.5), and we did not ablate it on its own. Real low-resolution samples also receive random affine and perspective warps, rotation up to $10^{\circ}$, brightness, contrast and hue jitter, and coarse dropout.

\subsection{Network Architecture}

\begin{figure}[!h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth,  height = 0.4\textheight, keepaspectratio]{Methonology/SystemOverview.png}
    \caption{The MF-SR-OCR pipeline.}
    \label{fig1}
\end{figure}

The network maps a tensor of shape $[B, 5, 3, 32, 128]$ to per-timestep class log-probabilities through the six stages numbered in Fig.~\ref{fig1}. Weights are shared across frames throughout, so the parameter count does not depend on how many frames a track contains.

\textbf{Stage 1 --- Straightening each frame.} A Spatial Transformer Network \cite{jaderberg2015stn} predicts six affine parameters $\theta_i$ per frame and resamples it. One detail of the localization network is worth calling out. It uses stride-1 convolutions with max-pooling and keeps a $4 \times 8$ spatial grid before the fully connected head instead of pooling down to $1 \times 1$. Rotation and translation are spatial quantities, and a descriptor that has been averaged over the whole image has very little left with which to express \emph{``the plate leans five degrees and sits left of centre''}; a head fed such a descriptor appears able to fit little beyond zoom. The last layer is initialized so that $\theta_i$ starts as the identity, which makes the module a no-op on the first pass and lets it learn a correction gradually.

\textbf{Stage 2 --- Aligning the frames to each other.} One affine warp per frame cannot describe local misalignment left over from motion blur, rolling shutter or a bent plate. We therefore align each frame toward the middle frame of its track using a modulated deformable convolution \cite{zhu2019dcnv2}, following the recipe that made this work for video restoration \cite{wang2019edvr}. Offsets and modulation masks are predicted from the frame and the reference stacked together, and both heads start at zero. Zeroing the offsets alone turned out not to be enough: with a randomly initialized $3\times3$ kernel the module replaces every RGB frame with a random mixture on the very first forward pass, so the kernel itself is initialized to the identity as well.

\textbf{Stage 3 --- Super-resolving the five frames together.} The restoration branch upscales the aligned frames by a factor of two. Shallow features are extracted per frame, averaged across the five to give a temporal context, and concatenated back onto each frame before a body of four residual blocks. Upsampling uses sub-pixel convolution, as adopted for plates in \cite{nascimento2023attention}, and the learned output is added on top of a bilinear upscale so the branch, like the rectifier before it, begins close to the identity. That temporal context is what makes the branch multi-frame rather than single-image, and at five to seven pixels of width per character it is the only extra signal plausibly available: a single frame simply does not contain more.

\textbf{Stage 4 --- The shared encoder.} A stem convolution feeds five residual stages with channel widths $(64, 128, 256, 256, 512)$, two blocks each, PReLU activations, squeeze-and-excitation channel re-weighting, and a residual scale of $0.1$ to keep long schedules stable. Following common practice in super-resolution we leave BatchNorm out of the residual blocks. Removing normalization altogether, however, did not survive contact with the SR head: once the spatial size doubles, feature scale drifts and the gradients reach NaN. GroupNorm \cite{wu2018groupnorm} restores the stability without reintroducing BatchNorm's dependence on batch size, since it normalizes each sample on its own. The stride schedule squeezes height down to a single row while protecting width, which becomes the CTC time axis: the $128$-pixel input is doubled by the $\times 2$ branch and then divided by eight, leaving $T = 32$ steps. During training we also replace whole frame feature maps with the track mean with probability $0.05$, so the model cannot lean on one unusually clear frame.

\textbf{Stage 5 --- Weighing the frames.} Frames inside a track differ in blur and compression, so they are not equally worth listening to. A small scoring network reduces each frame feature map to a single reliability score, a softmax over the five turns those into weights, and the fused map is the weighted sum.

\textbf{Stage 6 --- Reading the sequence.} The fused map is one row high, so its width axis can be read directly as a sequence by a two-layer bidirectional LSTM with $256$ hidden units, followed by LayerNorm, dropout and a linear projection to the $37$ classes. Training uses CTC \cite{graves2006ctc}, which learns the alignment between image and string without character segmentation, exactly as in the CRNN recognizer this pipeline extends \cite{shi2017crnn}. The full model has $29.58$M parameters.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\subsection{Joint Training Objective}

The six stages settle what the network computes. What it is asked to get right is a separate question, and until we answer it nothing forces the images leaving Stage~3 to look like plates at all: under the recognition loss alone that branch is free to emit whatever representation the encoder finds convenient. Supervising it against the high-resolution frames is what makes the pipeline jointly trained rather than merely sequential, so restoration and recognition share one loss:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Total}} = \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CTC}} + \lambda_{\mathrm{SR}} \cdot \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SR}},
\label{eq:total}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CTC}}$ is the transcription loss over the seven-character label and $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SR}}$ compares the restored frames against their high-resolution counterparts:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SR}} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}
  \left\| I_{\mathrm{SR}}^{(i)} - \mathrm{Warp}_{\mathrm{sg}[\theta_i]}\!\left(I_{\mathrm{HR}}^{(i)}\right) \right\|_1 ,
\label{eq:sr}
\end{equation}
with $N$ the number of frames in the batch that have a high-resolution target, and $\lambda_{\mathrm{SR}} = 0.1$ throughout.

Two details of Eq.~\eqref{eq:sr} are deliberate and easy to get wrong. The first is that only the \emph{target} is warped. Restoration runs after the rectifier, so $I_{\mathrm{SR}}$ is already expressed in the straightened frame while $I_{\mathrm{HR}}$ is not; warping both would apply the geometry twice, and warping neither would compare two different coordinate systems, in which case the branch can do little except blur. The second is the stop-gradient on $\theta_i$, written $\mathrm{sg}[\cdot]$. Without it the branch has an easy way to lower its own loss: pull the rectifier toward whichever geometry is simplest to reconstruct, and let the auxiliary task quietly take over the primary one. With it, the branch may sharpen the image but has no say in how the plate is straightened. The implementation also supports a VGG perceptual term and a Sobel edge term inside $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SR}}$; both weights are zero in every configuration reported here, so Eq.~\eqref{eq:sr} is exact for all of Section~3.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\subsection{Layout-Constrained CTC Decoding}

Everything above concerns training. One further choice belongs to inference. The CTC head from Stage~6 may emit any symbol at any position, and on this corpus most of that freedom is wasted: every label is seven characters long and follows one of two patterns, \texttt{LLLNNNN} or \texttt{LLLNLNN}. Those two differ only at position five, which means six of the seven positions have a class---letter or digit---that is fixed no matter which layout the plate belongs to. We therefore run a CTC prefix beam search of width $16$ and discard a prefix as soon as it violates both patterns. This happens entirely at decode time: no extra parameters, no retraining, and the training objective is untouched.

The \emph{w/o~SR} variant drops the second term of Eq.~\eqref{eq:total} along with the modules that produce it: the restoration branch, the deformable alignment, and the temporal averaging inside that branch. It isolates the restoration path as a whole. Attention fusion across frames stays, so this variant is still a multi-frame recognizer, not a single-image one. The \emph{SR$\times 1$} variant is the more subtle of the two. It keeps the branch but sets the upscaling factor to one, recovering the same $T = 32$ timesteps through a denser width schedule instead. Together the pair separates two effects that a naive comparison would confuse, since doubling the input width also doubles the CTC sequence length: a gain credited to super-resolution could just as easily come from having twice as many timesteps per character. The run identifiers in the last column of Table~\ref{tab:configs} are the internal names each configuration was trained and logged under, given only so the released logs can be matched to the table.

\subsection{Optimization and Evaluation Protocol}

Models are trained with AdamW at a peak learning rate of $8\times10^{-4}$ and weight decay $10^{-4}$, on a cosine schedule with $5\%$ linear warmup decaying to $5\%$ of the peak, with gradients clipped at global norm $2.0$ and mixed precision throughout. The effective batch size is $64$, reached as a micro-batch of $32$ with two accumulation steps. Runs last at most $60$ epochs and stop early after $18$ without improvement. We select the reported checkpoint by validation exact-match accuracy rather than validation loss, because the two disagree in practice: validation loss bottoms out around epoch $16$--$20$ and then climbs, while exact-match accuracy keeps improving until epoch $24$--$32$. An exponential moving average of the weights with decay $0.999$ \cite{tarvainen2017mean} runs throughout, and the averaged weights are the ones we evaluate.

The margins in this problem are small enough that a single run tells us very little. On $999$ validation tracks one track is worth $0.1$ percentage points, and the binomial standard deviation of a single measurement near $80\%$ accuracy is about $13$ tracks, so a one-point gap between two single runs is well inside what chance produces on its own. Every configuration is therefore trained with three seeds ($42$, $100$ and $2026$) and reported as mean $\pm$ standard deviation, with cuDNN autotuning switched off so a run can be repeated exactly. We treat a difference between two configurations as meaningful only when it exceeds twice the standard error of that difference.

Alongside exact match we report character error rate and normalized edit distance, which can separate configurations whose exact-match scores sit on top of each other, and---for configurations that contain the restoration branch---PSNR and SSIM of the super-resolved frames against the high-resolution ground truth. Absolute image-quality scores are hard to interpret on this corpus \cite{nascimento2025toward}, so we always report those two next to the bilinear upscale of the same input and read only the difference between them.

Two caveats come with this protocol. Early stopping picks the reported checkpoint on the same validation split we then measure on, which makes the absolute numbers mildly optimistic; comparisons between configurations remain sound, since every configuration is chosen and scored the same way on the same tracks. And every number in this paper is a validation number. The benchmark ships its public and blind test sets without labels, so no test accuracy can be computed locally. We did generate predictions for all $1{,}000$ public and $3{,}000$ blind tracks, from a single checkpoint---the seed-$42$ run of the full model, chosen because its score does not move when cuDNN autotuning is enabled---but we did not submit them, and no test-set result is claimed anywhere here. That checkpoint also happens to be the weakest of the three seeds on validation, so the choice cannot be read as picking the most flattering run.

The nine runs behind Section~3, three configurations at three seeds, cost $39.1$ GPU-hours in total on a single GPU: $5.6$~h for \emph{w/o~SR}, $22.2$~h for the full model and $11.3$~h for \emph{SR$\times 1$}. The restoration path accounts for most of that difference. It makes the proposed model $4.01\times$ more expensive to train, $4.17\times$ more expensive in floating-point operations per track, and $3.76\times$ slower at inference, $192.8$ against $51.3$~ms per track.

\section{Results and Discussion}

We start by putting the three configurations of Section~2.5 next to the baselines that ship with the competition. Then we ask the question the architecture was built around: does the restoration path earn what it costs? The answer is no, at least not here, and the two subsections that follow try to work out why. The first looks at the image-quality metrics that motivated the branch in the first place. The second looks at the mistakes the model actually makes.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

Table~\ref{tab:main} reports all three configurations over three seeds each. The full model reaches $79.95\% \pm 0.15$, roughly three points above the multi-frame CRNN+STN baseline it extends and above the stronger ResNet+Transformer+STN baseline as well. It is also the calmest of the three across seeds: its runs span $0.30$ points, against $0.90$ for the variant without restoration and $0.80$ for the one without upscaling.

The baseline rows come with a caveat. They are numbers released with the competition's baseline material\footnote{Baseline implementation and reported accuracies: \url{https://github.com/duongtruongbinh/MultiFrame-LPR}}, measured on the organizers' split rather than ours, so the comparison is indicative rather than exact. When we reproduced CRNN+STN on the split used here it scored about $75.8\%$, which would put the improvement nearer four points than three.

Set the baselines aside and the picture among our own three configurations is more sober. Section~2.6 asks a gap to clear twice the standard error of the difference before we call it real, and only one of the three pairwise comparisons manages it. The variant without upscaling is genuinely worse than the variant without restoration, by $0.97$ points against a standard error of $0.36$. The two comparisons that involve the full model do not clear the bar: $+0.50 \pm 0.28$ against \emph{w/o~SR} and $+0.47 \pm 0.27$ against \emph{SR$\times 1$}. On this data the full model and the variant that throws restoration away are indistinguishable.

The character error rates in Table~\ref{tab:main} rank the three the same way exact match does, but they are not simply saying it again. The variant without restoration leads on both, and it is the only configuration that never emitted a prediction of the wrong length: $0$, $0$ and $0$ length errors across its three seeds, against $0$, $1$, $0$ for the full model and $1$, $0$, $0$ for \emph{SR$\times 1$}. Where the two metrics part company is stability. \emph{SR$\times 1$} has the steadiest character error rate by a wide margin, $\pm 0.0001$ against $\pm 0.0016$ for the full model, and at the same time the least steady exact-match rate, $\pm 0.44$ against $\pm 0.15$. A model can be consistent about how many characters it gets wrong and erratic about which plates it gets entirely right. That is worth remembering whenever a single metric is used to rank models that are already close.

Constrained decoding adds very little on top of all this. Turning it off changes $0$, $2$ and $0$ tracks across the three seeds. It costs nothing at inference and never hurt a prediction in our runs, so we keep it, but it is not where the accuracy comes from.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\subsection{Does the Restoration Branch Justify Its Cost?}

The branch is doing real work. Fig.~\ref{fig:srloss} follows its reconstruction loss against a bilinear upscale of the same input, epoch by epoch. The learned path sits below interpolation at every one of the $46$ epochs, and the margin grows from $2.5\%$ at the start to $13.8\%$ by the end, so whatever Stage~3 has learned, it is not a slower way of doing what interpolation already does.

\begin{figure}[!h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Result/SRLossVsBilinear.png}
    \caption{Reconstruction loss of the learned branch against a bilinear upscale of the same input: (a) both losses per epoch, (b) their relative gap.}
    \label{fig:srloss}
\end{figure}

None of that seems to reach the reader. Take out the branch, its deformable alignment and the temporal averaging inside it, hold the backbone, the schedule and the baseline cluster fixed, and accuracy moves from $79.95\%$ to $80.45\%$. The move is inside the noise floor. Computation, meanwhile, falls from $109.08$ to $26.14$ GFLOPs per track, a factor of $4.17$. Looking track by track, the reduced variant is ahead at every seed, by $2$, $8$ and $5$ tracks. The direction is consistent, but the size of it is not something we can separate from chance. Training cost follows the same shape: $22.2$ GPU-hours for the full model against $5.6$ without the branch.

There are two ways to read this and our data does not choose between them. Restoration may simply not help this task, or our particular branch may be too weak and a stronger one would. What we can say is narrower: this branch, at this cost, buys nothing we can measure. The large multi-frame gains reported elsewhere \cite{na2025mflpr2,nascimento2025toward} came from different corpora and heavier restoration front-ends, so they neither contradict this nor settle it. What the result does undercut is the assumption we started with, that the improvement over the baseline came from super-resolution. Since the configuration with no restoration at all matches the full model, that improvement has to rest on the four settings all three share: the $4 \times 8$ localization grid, domain-matched degradation, constrained decoding and weight averaging. Taking that cluster apart is the obvious next experiment, and we leave it for future work.

The upscaling factor is a cleaner story. \emph{SR$\times 1$} keeps the branch but drops the $\times 2$ upscale, recovering the same $32$ CTC timesteps through a denser width schedule. It ends up $0.97$ points behind \emph{w/o~SR}, the one gap in Table~\ref{tab:main} that clears the significance bar. Doubling the input width, then, is not a free way to buy sequence length.

\subsection{Reconstruction Fidelity Is Not Recognition Performance}

Work on plate super-resolution has already reported that PSNR and SSIM follow recognition accuracy only loosely \cite{nascimento2025toward,tsai2025deep}. Our measurements push that further: here the two quantities move in opposite directions. Judged the conventional way the branch looks like a success. Against the high-resolution targets it gains $+0.72$~dB PSNR and $+0.043$ SSIM over the bilinear baseline, and \emph{SR$\times 1$} gains more still, $+1.04$~dB and $+0.076$. \emph{SR$\times 1$} is also the weakest recognizer of the three.

Fig.~\ref{fig:psnr} shows the inversion at track level. Panel~(a) splits the $999$ validation tracks by whether the model read them correctly. The misread tracks average $18.42$~dB against $16.12$~dB for the correct ones, so the failures score $2.30$~dB \emph{higher}, and the point-biserial correlation between PSNR and correctness comes out at $r = -0.41$. Panel~(b) puts the same tracks into PSNR quintiles, where exact-match accuracy falls all the way down from $93.5\%$ in the lowest quintile to $51.0\%$ in the highest. Running the same measurement on \emph{SR$\times 1$}, a different checkpoint at a different output resolution, gives $r = -0.40$ and a gap of $2.22$~dB. The effect looks like a property of the data rather than of one run.

\begin{figure}[!h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Result/PsnrVsCorrect.png}
    \caption{PSNR against readability on the $999$ validation tracks: (a) the two distributions, (b) exact-match accuracy per PSNR quintile.}
    \label{fig:psnr}
\end{figure}

A plausible explanation is that PSNR on this corpus is driven more by what is in the image than by how well it was restored. A blurred, low-contrast plate has almost no high-frequency detail to lose, so any reconstruction of it scores well while staying unreadable. A sharp plate carries exactly the fine detail that is hard to rebuild and easy to read. On that reading PSNR would be tracking how easy an image is to reconstruct, which on this data looks close to the opposite of how much readable evidence it holds. The implication is narrower than a claim about image-quality metrics in general. It is that on this data, choosing or supervising a model by PSNR alone would push it away from legibility rather than towards it. That is a concrete argument for the arrangement in Eq.~\eqref{eq:total}, where the reconstruction term answers to a recognition loss instead of standing on its own.

\subsection{Error Analysis}

The full model gets $202$ tracks wrong at seed $42$. Of those, $114$ differ from the ground truth in exactly one character, which is $56.4\%$, and another $18.3\%$ differ in two. This is the whole distance between the two metrics in Table~\ref{tab:main}. A character error rate of $0.054$ and an exact-match rate of $79.95\%$ describe the same predictions, one counting characters and the other refusing to give partial credit. It also says something about where the remaining headroom is. Most failures are not collapses but single confusions, usually between glyph pairs that survive downsampling badly.

Confidence is no help in finding them. Of the $202$ failures, $103$ carry a decoder confidence above $0.9$, and the mean confidence over all of them is $0.868$. A threshold set to catch errors would miss more than half. That is a warning for any deployment that plans to gate on the model's own certainty, and it matters for the benchmark too, whose tie-break rule is the confidence gap between correct and incorrect predictions. Over three seeds the full model separates those two groups by only $0.124 \pm 0.018$, or $0.990$ against $0.868$ at seed $42$. A ranking settled on that margin would rest on a quantity the model estimates poorly.

One last observation. About a fifth of the validation set depends on the seed. Comparing the three seeds of each configuration track by track, the predictions agree on $789$ of $999$ tracks for \emph{w/o~SR}, $778$ for the full model and $768$ for \emph{SR$\times 1$}. Every configuration changes $21$ to $23\%$ of its answers between otherwise identical runs. That is the strongest argument we can give for the multi-seed protocol of Section~2.6, and it is why we do not report any single run on its own.

\section{Conclusion}

We presented MF-SR-OCR, a multi-frame framework in which the restoration and the recognition of low-resolution license plates are trained under a single objective, with the reconstruction term supervised through a stop-gradient warp so that it can sharpen the image without steering the geometric rectifier. Over three seeds on $999$ authentic surveillance tracks the model reaches $79.95\% \pm 0.15$, above the multi-frame CRNN+STN baseline it extends and with the lowest seed-to-seed variance of the configurations we trained. A controlled ablation nonetheless removes the restoration path entirely and matches that accuracy at $4.17\times$ fewer floating-point operations, so on this benchmark the branch shows no measurable recognition gain---despite reconstructing more faithfully than bilinear interpolation at every epoch of training.

Two observations follow from that result. Reconstruction fidelity and readability are not merely uncorrelated on this data but opposed: the tracks the model misreads score $2.30$~dB higher in PSNR than the ones it reads correctly, and exact-match accuracy falls monotonically across PSNR quintiles. And the margins separating architectural variants here are no larger than the variation between random seeds, which leaves single-run comparison uninformative. Taken together, the improvement over the baseline appears to rest on geometric rectification, domain-matched degradation, constrained decoding and weight averaging rather than on super-resolution. Decomposing that cluster, and comparing against a modern scene-text recognizer such as SVTRv2 \cite{du2025svtrv2}, are the two experiments we would run next.
